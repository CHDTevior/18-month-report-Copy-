\chapter{Summary of Work}

\section{Research narrative}

This project asks how a generative model of human motion can be made both
\emph{precisely controllable} and \emph{general} --- general across the bodies
it animates and across the instructions it is given. The work to date traces a
single line: each piece exposed a limit in the previous one, and the limit
defined the next problem.

The starting point was the representation gap. Text is symbolic and sparse;
motion is continuous, high-dimensional and constrained by kinematics. The first
work (MCRE) closed this gap by training a lightweight motion adapter against a
frozen pre-trained text encoder~\cite{radford2021learning}, placing captions and
motions in one conditional space in which semantic edits appear as approximately
linear directions. This provided the conditioning interface that every
subsequent piece of work reuses, but it also exposed the first limit: a single
vector describes a whole clip, so control is available only at the granularity
of an entire sentence.

The second limit was temporal. Diffusion-based text-to-motion
models~\cite{tevet2022human,chen2023executing,zhang2024motiondiffuse} produce
high-fidelity clips, but iterative sampling is slow and the clip length is
fixed at training time, which rules out interactive use. MOGO addressed this by
discretising motion with a scale-adaptive residual vector
quantizer~\cite{vandenoord2017neural,zeghidour2022soundstream} and decoding all
quantization levels in one causal forward pass. The result is real-time,
unbounded in length, and re-promptable mid-stream. The cost is that motion now
lives on a finite codebook: quantization error places a floor on achievable
fidelity, and that floor is not uniform --- it is largest exactly where
precision matters most, at extremities and at contact.

This observation produced the reframing that organises the current phase of the
project. Up to this point, every attempt at control had been an attempt to
\emph{generate} the desired motion by strengthening the condition. But a
stronger condition buys better \emph{expected} behaviour, never a guarantee: a
model asked to place a foot at a particular point will place it approximately
there. The third work (CodeGeo) therefore treats editing not as generation but
as \emph{projection}. Given a motion and a set of geometric residuals ---
a joint that must reach a target, a foot that must not slide --- the correction
is computed as a metric-weighted least-norm step in the quantizer's latent
space, which satisfies the constraints while remaining on the learned manifold.
Control granularity drops from the sentence to the individual joint and contact
event, and it becomes exact rather than approximate.

At that point the remaining limits were no longer about precision but about
generality, and there are two of them. First, all of the above assumes one
body. HumanML3D~\cite{guo2022generating}, KIT-ML~\cite{plappert2016kitkit} and
AMASS~\cite{mahmood2019amass} share a single SMPL~\cite{loper2015smpl} skeleton,
and a model trained on it transfers to a different character only through
retargeting~\cite{aberman2020skeletonaware}, which presupposes a correspondence
that may not exist. The fourth work (TopX) removes this assumption, generating
motion for skeletons of arbitrary structure without a fixed reference topology.
Second, all of the above assumes one instruction. Real requests are composite
and ordered --- walk to the table, pick up the cup, turn around --- and
concatenating independently generated clips produces visible seams and
inconsistent style~\cite{shafir2024priormdm,barquero2024flowmdm}. The fifth
work addresses multi-subtask generation directly.

These two threads define the remaining period. For TopX the question is
out-of-distribution specialisation: a topology-agnostic model is only
interesting if it holds up on skeletons genuinely unlike those it was trained
on, and the current model does not yet. For multi-subtask generation the
question is how subtask boundaries should be represented so that composition is
learned rather than stitched. Both are described in Chapter~3.

One line of work from the 18-month report, MotionDirector, is not carried
forward as a thesis chapter. It investigated video-prior dual conditioning with
a cross-modal fusion module and a distribution-alignment objective. The
experiments were informative --- in particular about how far a conditioning
signal can be strengthened before returns diminish --- and that negative result
contributed directly to the reframing described above, but the work relied on
rendered rather than real video and was judged not to constitute a distinct
contribution. Its useful components are absorbed into Chapters~3 and 5.

\section{Completed contributions}

\subsection{Shared conditional representation for text and motion (MCRE)}

Text-to-motion models must reconcile two modalities that differ in
dimensionality, length and structure. Most systems handle this by conditioning
a generator on a text embedding produced by an encoder that has never seen
motion, which leaves the two representations only loosely coupled and makes
any operation other than one-shot generation --- editing, interpolation,
composition --- difficult to express.

This work trained a lightweight motion adapter against a frozen pre-trained
text encoder, so that a motion and its caption map to nearby points in a single
conditional space. Because the space is shared and the alignment objective is
contrastive, semantic relationships between captions carry over to motions:
edits such as changing the speed, the direction or the affect of a movement can
be applied as displacement vectors in the conditional space rather than by
re-prompting the generator, and multiple conditions can be combined by
composition in the same space.

The contribution is less a competitive generator than an interface. It
established the conditioning mechanism reused throughout the thesis, and it
made the granularity problem explicit: a clip-level embedding can express
\emph{what} a motion is, but not \emph{where} or \emph{when} any part of it
should happen. That observation motivates the later chapters.

This work was published at the ECCV 2024 Workshop on Foundation Models for
3D Humans~\cite{sun2024mcre}.

\subsection{Hierarchical one-pass autoregressive generation (MOGO)}

Diffusion-based motion models set a high bar for fidelity and diversity, but
their iterative sampling makes them unsuitable for interactive use, and their
fixed-horizon training makes long-form generation awkward. Tokenization-based
models~\cite{chuan2022tm2t,guo2024momask} decode faster, but residual
quantization schemes typically require one decoding pass per quantization
level, and error accumulates over long autoregressive rollouts.

This work introduced two components. MoSA-VQ is a residual vector quantizer
with learnable per-level scaling and a cross-level decorrelation objective; the
decorrelation term minimises the batch-wise covariance between a quantized
vector and the residual passed to the next level, which enforces approximate
statistical orthogonality across the hierarchy so that each level encodes
information the previous levels did not. This improves codebook utilisation and
yields more compact codes under a fixed budget. The RQHC-Transformer is a
causal decoder whose structure mirrors the quantization hierarchy and which
emits all levels in a single forward pass, removing the per-level decoding cost
while preserving temporal coherence.

Together these give near real-time decoding and generation of unbounded length,
with the ability to inject a new text prompt at an arbitrary point in the
sequence --- the property that makes the system usable for avatar control and
teleoperation rather than offline clip synthesis. A textual condition alignment
module normalises free-form user input into structured instructions before
generation, which improves robustness on prompts unlike those seen in training.

Ablations confirmed both components: removing the decorrelation objective
degraded generation FID and retrieval accuracy consistently across metrics, and
codebook capacity showed that the configuration minimising reconstruction error
is not the one maximising generation quality --- a gap that is itself evidence
that quantization error is structured rather than uniform noise, and which
motivated the next work.

An earlier form of the decoder was published at CVMP 2025 as
Hi-RQCT~\cite{sun2025hirqct}; the full system was published at AAAI
2026~\cite{sun2025mogo}.

\subsection{Constrained correction in discrete latent space (CodeGeo)}

Both of the preceding works control motion by conditioning: the model is told
what is wanted and produces something close to it. For a large class of
practical requirements this is not enough. A foot that must remain planted, a
hand that must reach a specific point, a trajectory that must pass through a
waypoint --- these are constraints, and a conditional model satisfies them only
in expectation. Increasing guidance strength trades one failure for another,
tightening the constraint at the cost of diversity and, past a point, of
plausibility.

This work reframes the problem. A generated motion is treated as a point in the
quantizer's latent space, and each requirement is written as a geometric
residual that should be driven to zero. The correction is then the least-norm
step, under a metric derived from the learned representation, that eliminates
the residual to first order --- a damped, metric-weighted projection of the
form
\[
  \Delta \;=\; -\,\mathbf{M}\mathbf{J}^{\top}
  \bigl(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{J}^{\top}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{r},
\]
where $\mathbf{J}$ is the Jacobian of the residual with respect to the latent,
$\mathbf{M}$ is the metric and $\lambda$ damps the solution near singular
configurations. Choosing $\mathbf{M}$ from the statistics of the learned
codebook rather than as the identity is what keeps the correction on the motion
manifold: the step is cheapest in the directions the data actually varies
along, so the edit borrows from the model's prior instead of fighting it.

The practical consequences are that constraints are satisfied exactly rather
than approximately; that the edit is local, leaving unconstrained parts of the
motion untouched; and that the procedure is applied after generation, so it
composes with any generator that exposes a latent space --- including MOGO.
This is the point at which control in the thesis becomes exact rather than
statistical, and it completes the discrete-representation line of work.

This work is currently under review at AAAI.

\subsection{Topology-agnostic motion generation (TopX)}

Every model described so far is trained on a single skeleton. The standard
benchmarks all derive from SMPL~\cite{loper2015smpl}, and a model that has
learned motion on that skeleton can animate a different character only by
retargeting~\cite{aberman2020skeletonaware}, which assumes a joint
correspondence between source and target. For characters whose morphology
differs substantially --- different limb counts, different proportions,
non-humanoid structure --- that correspondence does not exist, and the
assumption fails silently rather than visibly.

This work generates motion for skeletons of arbitrary topology without a fixed
reference structure, so that the skeleton is an input to the model rather than
a property of the dataset.
