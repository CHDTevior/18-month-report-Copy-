\chapter{Thesis Structure}

\section{Introduction}

Motion generation produces movement for virtual characters, avatars and robots
from high-level descriptions. Text is the most natural of these descriptions: a
user states what a character should do, and the model produces the motion.
Text-to-motion generation now serves animation, games, virtual production,
telepresence and embodied agents, where motion has to be produced on demand
rather than captured.

\section{Problem definition}

Given a text description, and optionally further conditions such as a target
trajectory or a target skeleton, text-to-motion generation produces a motion
sequence consistent with all of them. Practical use places two demands on this
task. The first is \emph{controllability}: animators and downstream systems
specify motion at the level of a named joint, a path or a time interval, so the
generator must satisfy such constraints precisely, in real time and for
sequences of any length. The second is \emph{generality}: the same generator
should serve characters with different skeletons, and requests that combine
several instructions.

\section{Research goals}

The goal of this thesis is a text-to-motion generator that is controllable at
the level of individual joints and general across bodies and tasks. The thesis
pursues this goal through five research questions, each answered by one
contribution chapter:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*}, leftmargin=3em]
\item How can text and motion share one conditioning space that supports
  semantic editing? (Chapter~3)
\item How can motion be generated in real time, at unbounded length, with
  prompts that change during generation? (Chapter~4)
\item How can precise spatial control be added to a pretrained text-to-motion
  generator without retraining it? (Chapter~5)
\item How can one model generate text-conditioned motion for skeletons of
  arbitrary topology? (Chapter~6)
\item How can one model generate long-horizon motion from ordered, composite
  instructions? (Chapter~7)
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Summary of Work}

\section{Research narrative}

This thesis builds a text-to-motion system that is controllable at the
granularity practitioners work with --- a named joint, a sketched path, a
specific interval --- and general enough to carry over to a new body or a new
task. The work reaches this through one representational commitment: motion is
represented as \emph{addressable tokens}, per body part and then per joint of
any skeleton, and control is expressed as an operation on those tokens rather
than as a stronger condition on a generator.

The first work, MCRE~\cite{sun2024mcre}, builds a shared conditioning space for
text and motion. A lightweight motion adapter aligned to a frozen text
encoder~\cite{radford2021learning} places captions and motions in one space, so
that semantic edits correspond to linear directions in that space.

The second work makes generation fast and unbounded. Diffusion text-to-motion
models~\cite{tevet2022human,chen2023executing,zhang2024motiondiffuse} sample
iteratively and train at a fixed horizon, which rules out interactive use.
Hi-RQCT~\cite{sun2025hirqct} and then MOGO~\cite{sun2025mogo} (AAAI 2026) discretize motion
with a scale-adaptive residual vector
quantizer~\cite{vandenoord2017neural,zeghidour2022soundstream} and decode every
quantization level in a single causal forward pass, giving real-time synthesis,
unbounded sequence length and mid-stream prompt switching. This establishes the
discrete substrate on which Chapters~4 and 5 build.

The third work adds precise spatial control. Placing one hand at one point, or a
root along a sketched path, calls for tokens that name individual body parts.
This work treats an anatomy-aligned part codebook (PartVQ) as a control surface
and uses it twice: KV-Control feeds a target trajectory into a frozen generator
through its attention layers, at the tokens of the relevant body parts, and
CodeGeo then corrects the result in a few analytic steps measured in the
codebook's own geometry. An ablation shows that the control accuracy comes from
the part-tokenized representation rather than from the injection alone.

Because control accuracy comes from the representation, the fourth work carries
that representation beyond a single body. Standard motion datasets such as
HumanML3D~\cite{guo2022generating} share one human
skeleton~\cite{loper2015smpl}, while games, film and virtual production include
many characters that are not human, each rigged with its own joint count, naming
and helper bones. TopX carries the addressable-token idea from one body to any
body: each token is one joint at one frame, any rigged animation is converted to
a shared per-joint representation, and the skeleton enters the model as a
prompt, so nothing in the network is specific to one rig. This follows the same
principle as KV-Control: what the model must obey is made addressable inside
attention rather than built into the architecture. One model serves 311 animal
rigs, and an unseen rig is served by the same forward pass.

Two directions follow. The first extends TopX from its animal library to
arbitrary skeletons --- humans, animals and fantasy creatures --- including
skeletons never seen in training. The second extends generation from one
instruction to ordered, composite requests. Both are set out in Chapter~3.

\section{Completed contributions}

\subsection{Shared conditional representation for text and motion}

Text-to-motion models must reconcile modalities that differ in dimensionality,
length and structure. Conditioning a generator on an embedding from an encoder
that has never seen motion couples the two representations loosely, which makes
editing difficult to express.

This work trains a lightweight motion adapter aligned to a frozen pretrained
text encoder, mapping a motion and its caption to nearby points in one
conditional space. In this shared space, semantic edits correspond to linear
directions, so a motion is edited by moving its representation rather than by
re-prompting the generator.

Published at the ECCV 2024 Workshop on Foundation Models for 3D
Humans~\cite{sun2024mcre}.

\subsection{Hierarchical one-pass autoregressive generation}

Diffusion models sample iteratively and train at a fixed horizon, and
token-based models usually decode one quantization level at a time. This work
discretizes motion with a scale-adaptive residual quantizer (MoSA-VQ), whose
cross-level decorrelation objective lets each level encode what the previous
levels did not, and decodes all levels in one causal forward pass
(RQHC-Transformer). The result is real-time generation of unbounded length, in
which a new text prompt can be given at any point in the sequence, which makes
the system usable for avatar control and teleoperation. A text alignment module
turns free-form user input into structured instructions before generation.

The decoder was published at CVMP 2025 as Hi-RQCT~\cite{sun2025hirqct}; the full
system was published at AAAI 2026 as MOGO~\cite{sun2025mogo}.

\subsection{Part-addressable control and codebook-geometry correction}

A text prompt does not let a user fix where a joint goes. Existing trajectory
controllers either add a near-copy of the generator's backbone, as in
OmniControl~\cite{xie2024omnicontrol} and
MaskControl~\cite{pinyoanuntapong2025maskcontrol}, or rely on long test-time
optimization. This work makes the generator's part codebook the control
surface. PartVQ gives each body part its own codebook, so every token names a
body part at a frame; KV-Control feeds the target trajectory into the frozen
generator as extra attention memory at those tokens, training only a small
adapter; and CodeGeo corrects the result in at most eight analytic steps
measured in the codebook's own geometry, in place of a 1,600-iteration
refinement. The work also gives a rule for setting that geometry according to
how the target joints are spread across the body.

The method tracks pelvis and multi-joint trajectories to sub-centimetre accuracy
and improves on MaskControl in both tracking accuracy and motion quality. This
work is under review at ICLR 2027.

\subsection{Skeleton-aware text-to-motion across heterogeneous topologies}

Text-to-motion models are built for one skeleton, so every new character needs
a new model or a retargeting step~\cite{aberman2020skeletonaware}. TopX makes
the skeleton an input. Any rigged animation is converted to a shared per-joint
representation (TopX-17) from its kinematic tree, rest pose and motion alone; the
skeleton enters the model as a prompt, through its rest pose and the distances
in its joint tree; and a grouped flow-matching objective balances training
across bodies of very different sizes. Nothing in the network is specific to
one rig.

Trained on 311 animal rigs with 34 to 102 joints and 77,894 captioned
clips~\cite{wang2025animo}, one model reaches retrieval accuracy close to that
of real motion (R@1 0.867 against 0.904) and outputs joint rotations and a root
trajectory that play directly on the rig. Rigs outside the library are served
zero-shot by the same forward pass, and few-shot with a small adapter trained on
a handful of clips. This work is under review at ICLR 2027.

\subsection{Collaborative work}

Three further motion systems were developed with collaborators.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{MotionDuet}~\cite{zhang2026motionduet} (CVPR 2026) aligns
  text-conditioned motion generation with the distribution of video-derived
  representations through dual text--video conditioning. I was responsible for
  bridging the video feature extractor into the model and for the training
  design.
\item \textbf{MoGeFlow}~\cite{fang2026mogeflow} generates text-conditioned motion
  as a continuous flow through motion-codebook geometry rather than by
  categorical code prediction. I contributed to the design of the DiT backbone
  and to the ablations on the role of the codebook at inference.
\item \textbf{UMO}~\cite{zhan2026umo} models sparse motion in a unified framework
  for real-time co-speech avatars. I was responsible for the data format, the
  motion-control training and the design of the motion-editing training
  framework.
\end{itemize}
